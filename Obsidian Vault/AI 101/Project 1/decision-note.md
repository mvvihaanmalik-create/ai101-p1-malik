# Decision Note

## Keep

I kept `AI101_P1_Malik_Vihaan_Poster.png`. The final poster makes the exhibited AI artwork, rather than the student, the visual focus. A cropped blue student at the left edge acts as a scale cue and pins up one dominant coral work. Inside that work, a profile changes through repeated contour lines, a node structure, and a resolved solid silhouette. Those three states belong to one system, so the poster feels experimental without becoming random. The large cream field, asymmetrical crop, and absence of furniture or studio props make it read as an exhibition poster instead of a scene illustration. I also posterised the final raster by hand to the exact palette—ultramarine `#2145D9`, coral `#FF5A4F`, and cream `#FFF1D0`—so the original three-colour rule is true at the pixel level.

## Reject

I rejected `poster-flat-three-color.svg`, the first output from the bare prompt. It met the measurable constraints: it was square, used three colours, contained bold flat shapes, and had no text. However, its discs, mast, and bands were only generic Bauhaus decoration. Nothing established a student, AI artwork, a pin-up, or an exhibition. This rejection showed that formal compliance alone was not enough; the image needed a clear subject and a reason for its shapes.

## First-to-final change and the steering move

The work changed from an abstract arrangement with no readable subject into an artwork-centred exhibition poster. Several intermediate rounds added a student, gallery panels, tools, plants, and studio details, but that made the result feel like a polished character illustration. The decisive steering move was the Round 9 instruction: stop treating the student as the hero, reduce the person to a cropped curatorial silhouette, and make one coherent AI artwork occupy most of the square. I also asked that every abstract element show a stage of the same transformation rather than function as decoration. That instruction created the final hierarchy and gave the abstraction a visible logic.

## Comparison observation

The visible difference between the early and later outputs traces back to context and direction rather than to the shared one-line prompt. With the bare prompt, the model defaulted to generic abstract poster shapes. When it could see the Russian Contemporary Art Week, Pole, Open Cosmos, and gallery references—and when I explicitly named the jobs of negative space, monumental massing, repetition, and human scale—the result became more like a contemporary exhibition poster. The initial Codex pass also leaned toward a detailed lifestyle illustration, while the later passes became more restrained only after I prohibited plants, tools, room scenery, and character detail. The comparison suggests that references establish a visual vocabulary, but explicit exclusions and hierarchy instructions determine which parts of that vocabulary actually survive into the final image.
